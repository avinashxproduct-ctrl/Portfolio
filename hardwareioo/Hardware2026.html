IBM Power HMC - A complex product, evolving experience

IBM Power servers run banks, airlines, and telcos. The people who manage them spend their days inside PowerHMC — a dense, enterprise-grade console.

The context

A mature enterprise product

PowerHMC supports administration of IBM Power systems. The experience includes legacy UI patterns and established infrastructure-management workflows.

The design challenge

Modernize without losing the workflow

Translate existing tasks and information structures into a more consistent experience aligned with React and Carbon Design System—while validating what should change and what must remain.

My contribution

From audit to implementation

Legacy UI analysis, workflow and IA mapping, sketches and wireframes, Carbon-aligned design, sponsor-user feedback, cross-functional decisions and implementation review.

Before the problem: how the pieces fit

PowerHMC is the control panel for a Power server. To understand “Hardware virtualized I/O”, it helps to see who is connected to what.

In one sentence: one physical network connection enters the server, the VIOS virtualizes it, and many partitions each use their own slice of it for their workloads — like one water main feeding a building, with a pump room sharing it out to every flat. Hardware virtualized I/O is the HMC area where admins see and manage that adapter, its ports and how they are shared out — the screen this case study redesigns.

Problem Identification

Why is the HMC interface changing?

The problem is bigger than one feature. Hardware virtualized I/O is one of many screens moving to a new platform.

Before

A patchwork built over 30 years

It started with a custom-built language, then moved through upgrades to Vaadin and Dojo. Over time these became outdated, hard to maintain and inconsistent for users.

Now

React + Carbon

Everything is being rebuilt with React for the frontend and the Carbon Design System for components. The result is clean, consistent and scalable.

How

Micro-frontends

Instead of one giant application, the UI is split into smaller pieces, each owned by a different team and plugged into one central dashboard. This makes updates faster and easier to test.

For design, this means one consistent, responsive experience for enterprise users, built on Carbon, flexible enough to plug into a modular structure and ready for incremental rollout.

Everything happened inside a modal

Hardware virtualized I/O shows what the legacy patchwork felt like. Managing SR-IOV in the legacy PowerHMC meant picking an adapter from a dropdown, then navigating through a chain of centered pop-up dialogs — each covering the table you came from.

Hidden information

The page was empty until you chose an adapter from a dropdown. No overview, no status, no comparison.

Modal on modal

Every edit — adapter mode, port settings, logical port details — opened a centered dialog that obscured the context behind it.

No system feedback

No loading states. No progress during mode changes. No error or success outcomes. Users couldn't tell waiting from failing.

02

Every edit happens in a centered modal & Nothing tells you what the system is doing

The issue

Want to change an adapter from Shared mode to Dedicated? A modal appears over the page. It shows two radio buttons and grey OK / Cancel buttons. The modal covers the very port table you were just looking at. There's no visual weight to the primary action — OK looks the same as Cancel.

Mode changes — switching an adapter from Shared to Dedicated — are slow operations on the server. They can take time. But none of the legacy screens showed a loading state, a progress indicator, a success confirmation, or an error message. Users clicked OK and waited. They had no way to distinguish "it's running" from "it failed silently."

Investigate & prioritize

Seven sessions. Real admins.

The project included four discovery sessions and three design-feedback sessions. These weren't exclusively about SR-IOV — they covered the broader feature context. But several signals landed directly on what I was designing.

4 discovery research sessions

We started by listening. We wanted to learn what users do, how they operate key features, how they experience the legacy panels today, and whether they need anything new in their workflow.

From their answers, we mapped an as-is story for each user. We then pulled out the insights that were unique to each person, instead of repeating what everyone said. Those insights became need statements, pain points and actionable items.

Finally, we took these findings to the product managers and the engineering team. Together we decided what was in scope and what was not. For anything left out, we agreed on the reason and on when it could be considered, or whether it would not be considered at all.

3 evaluative research (feedback) sessions

The designs were shaped by the discovery sessions and the legacy audits.

Once they reached high-fidelity screens, we went back to the same users to see whether the designs worked for them.

We gave them tasks to complete and asked what they would do to finish each one. We observed, mapped their steps, and asked specific questions along the way to gather their feedback.

As before, we captured each user's individual insights along with the unique ones, and used them to move the design forward.

Design & deliver

Decisions, each traced to its evidence

01

Show all adapters upfront - not behind a dropdown

The solution

The old page was blank until you chose. You couldn't compare adapters, see their status, or understand which one needed attention without working through the dropdown one entry at a time.

The redesign lands directly on a table. All adapters, with their mode, status and location code visible at once.

EvidenceFeedback user 2: "This is definitely better." Users prefer the new view because it surfaces adapter information upfront and makes selection easier.

02

A side panel with sticky Cancel / Save, Design waiting, failing and succeeding

The solution

The redesign opens a right-hand side panel from a selected row. The table stays visible. The Cancel and Save buttons are pinned to the bottom — they never scroll away.

The redesign treats each state as a first-class design problem: skeleton loading, a missing-adapter warning with disabled fields, an in-progress state with a progress bar and estimated duration, and explicit error and success outcomes.

EvidenceFeedback user 3: "This is easier to read and easier to understand."

03

Progressive disclosure on the adapter page

The solution

On the adapter page, both sections used to expand by default. This pushed content down and created visual noise.

I chose to expand Physical ports and collapse Logical ports — because research suggested logical-port creation is done at partition level, and admins change them infrequently. Physical ports are the more common working context.

EvidenceResearch backed data: Research suggests logical-port creation is done at partition level and admins use this infrequently.

Overall impact

↗

Visible

Visibility of system status

Legacy screens showed no progress, error or success. The redesign has 5 states: skeleton, in progress, error, success and missing.

↗

Findable

Findability

Adapters were hidden behind a dropdown. They are now listed upfront in a table, with a Negotiated speed column.

↗

Direct

Interaction cost

Radio, then a one-item Action menu, then a modal. Now it is one link into a port page.

↗

In context

Context retention

Centered modals covered the table being edited. A side panel now keeps the page visible.

↗

Consistent

Consistency and standards

Carbon and Vaadin-style controls were mixed. Now it is Carbon throughout, with sentence case and the same table tools.

↗

Oriented

Wayfinding

Port detail had no breadcrumb or return point. Each port now has its own page with a breadcrumb.

Based on a heuristic review of legacy screens against the redesign. Not a usability test.

Reflection & next

What this project taught me, and what comes after the first release.

What I learned

Collaborate at every step

Working closely with Product Managers and the Engineering team at every step helped me understand the business point of view, what was feasible, what was achievable, and how to scope it.

Sponsor user research

Users shaped the design

Sessions with sponsor users showed which legacy behaviours mattered. They preferred seeing adapter information upfront, expected IDs to be clickable, and asked to see the real link speed.

What I'd do differently

Prototype states earlier

Loading and error states surfaced late. Next time I would design them alongside the happy path instead of after it.

What's next

Extend the pattern

Apply the same side-panel and feedback patterns to the remaining PowerHMC workflows, and validate the shipped experience with users.
